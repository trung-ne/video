SOUTH KOREA'S LEVERAGE FEVER: WHY SO MANY PEOPLE BORROW TO INVEST
English translation of the provided Vietnamese video transcript

When people think of South Korea, they often think of kimchi, romantic dramas, and youthful-looking oppas. But there is another issue that has become an increasingly serious headache: more and more people believe that investing is no longer about building wealth slowly over time. Instead, they see it as the quickest way to transform their lives.

Stocks, cryptocurrencies, real estate—anything with the potential to rise in price attracts people eager to jump in. And what makes this game so much more dangerous is margin, or, more simply, borrowed money used for investing.

The consequences became clear in early 2026, when South Korea's KOSPI index plunged by more than 18% in just a few days, wiping hundreds of billions of dollars off the market—one of its sharpest declines in years. What stood out, however, was that many investors were using extremely high leverage. Some securities firms even had to temporarily suspend margin lending because they had reached their lending limits.

But why did this happen in a market that is far from an emerging one? South Korea is one of Asia's largest economies. It has weathered numerous financial crises, and its people are famous for being competitive and disciplined in their work. So why are so many people willing to borrow money to bet on the market? And why has leverage become so popular?

That's what we're going to explore in today's video. I'm Nheo, and this is the story of South Korea's leverage fever.

First, we need to understand what leverage—or margin trading—actually is. Let's have a quick refresher.

Margin trading, simply put, means borrowing money from a brokerage to buy additional shares. Say you have 100 million Vietnamese dong, and the brokerage lends you another 100 million. Instead of buying 100 million dong worth of stocks, you can now buy 200 million dong worth.

If the share price rises by 10%, you make about 20 million dong rather than 10 million. That's the basic idea. But borrowing money does not guarantee that the market will rise. If the shares fall by 10%, your loss is also doubled. Meanwhile, you still have to repay the loan, along with all the interest.

In a way, margin trading is like fitting a racing engine to your investment portfolio. If you're heading in the right direction, it's fantastic. But one sharp turn can send you flying off the track.

And the most dangerous part is something called a margin call. When the value of the assets in your account falls below the required safety threshold, the brokerage demands that you deposit more money. If you cannot do so in time, the firm has the right to sell your shares to recover its loan, regardless of whether the price is favorable or terrible at that moment.

Here's where things usually go wrong. During a market crash, tens of thousands of people can receive margin calls at once. They all sell, which pushes prices down even further. As prices fall, more people receive margin calls, triggering yet another round of selling.

Imagine a line of people standing on a staircase. All it takes is for one person to fall, and many of the people behind them may tumble too.

And that is essentially what happened in South Korea this year.

But the roots of this crash lie in the way an entire generation has come to view investing and getting rich.

South Korea currently has one of the highest household-debt ratios in the world. Over the past two decades, household debt has continued to grow and is now equivalent to almost the country's entire GDP. By the end of 2025, total household credit was approaching 2,000 trillion won—an enormous amount.

What is particularly important is that a large share of this debt is connected to housing.

South Korea has a rather unusual rental system called jeonse. Instead of paying rent every month, a tenant gives the landlord a very large lump-sum deposit. In many cases, that deposit can be worth as much as half the value of the home. In return, the tenant can live there for several years without paying monthly rent.

It sounds reasonable, but there is a problem: the upfront payment is huge. To rent a place, you already need a substantial amount of money.

When interest rates were low, this arrangement could work quite well. Money was cheap. Landlords could invest the deposits, while tenants did not have to make monthly rent payments. Everybody seemed happy.

But when interest rates rose, many landlords began struggling to return those deposits when leases expired. And the people who felt the pressure first were often the tenants.

If you're young, the situation is even harder. The employment rate among young people is considerably lower than in many other developed economies. Many work on short-term contracts, part-time schedules, or in otherwise unstable jobs.

As a result, their incomes rise very slowly, while living costs and asset prices keep climbing. That creates a widespread feeling among young South Koreans: even if you study hard, get a decent education, and work diligently, the life your parents' generation achieved seems to be moving further and further out of reach.

Buying a house is harder. Building wealth is harder. And the gap just keeps widening.

Against that backdrop, financial markets start to look extremely attractive. It isn't simply that people want to get rich quickly. Many feel that they don't have many other choices.

If wages grow more slowly than asset prices, working hard no longer seems like enough. You start looking for other paths. The stock market begins to look like a shortcut, and margin trading is like being handed a faster motorcycle to take down that shortcut.

But greater speed also means greater risk.

Young South Koreans have a telling way of describing this feeling: the sense of being left behind. Everything around them seems to be accelerating—home prices, asset prices, and the wealth of people who already own assets—while they themselves remain stuck in the same place.

When that feeling spreads across an entire generation, it begins to shape financial decisions. People jump into the market after reading a few investment books, or simply because they feel that time is chasing them. House prices are chasing them. Living expenses are chasing them. And if they don't run faster, the gap will only get bigger.

But that is only half the story. The other half lies in the structure of South Korea's stock market.

One unusual feature of the South Korean market is the enormous role played by individual investors. In many developed markets, much of the daily trading comes from investment funds, banks, and large financial institutions. Retail investors are still there, of course, but they are not usually the main force determining short-term market trends.

In South Korea, however, individual investors have accounted for a large share of market turnover for years. In 2020, they represented roughly two-thirds of trading volume. Even in 2026, their share has frequently hovered around 60%.

In other words, about six out of every ten trades come from individual investors. That is a very high proportion, and it has an important consequence: when retail investors get excited, huge amounts of money can rush into the market. When they panic, selling pressure can arrive just as quickly.

The market therefore becomes especially sensitive to crowd psychology.

South Koreans even have a nickname for these individual investors: gaemi, meaning 'ants.' An ant may be tiny, but if a million ants all move in the same direction, they can generate a surprisingly powerful force—especially when many of them are carrying financial leverage.

Research on investment behavior in South Korea has identified a number of familiar mistakes among individual investors. They are easily influenced by the crowd. They buy because they see others buying. They experience FOMO—the fear of missing out—when a stock starts climbing rapidly.

Many also enjoy hunting for small, high-risk stocks that might rise in price very quickly. It's a bit like buying a lottery ticket and hoping to hit the jackpot.

Another common tendency is selling profitable investments too early while holding on to losing ones, hoping the price will eventually recover. This is a well-known phenomenon in behavioral finance, and it is certainly not unique to South Korea. Investors around the world make the same mistakes.

But in South Korea, those tendencies become more extreme when margin trading enters the picture, because the people using margin are often among the most active traders.

According to research by the Korea Capital Market Institute, or KCMI, margin users make up only a small proportion of individual investors, yet they generate a disproportionately large number of trades.

They trade more frequently, prefer more volatile stocks, hold more concentrated portfolios, and accept higher levels of risk. In other words, they are the people pressing down hardest on the accelerator.

The problem is that their cars are running on borrowed money.

When the market is doing well, everything seems fine. They may make more money than everyone else. But when the market crashes, they tend to be among the first to get into trouble—and the consequences can be especially devastating.

Ironically, once you add up all the costs—trading fees, margin interest, and investment losses—this group's results are often no better than those of ordinary investors. In many cases, they are actually worse.

They take greater risks, experience more stress, and trade more often, yet the final outcome is not necessarily any better. That is one of the greatest paradoxes of South Korea's margin-trading fever.

So why are so many South Korean investors willing to make such enormous bets? There are several reasons.

The first is the pressure to accumulate wealth. As we discussed earlier, when house prices rise too quickly and the traditional path of saving becomes increasingly unrealistic, people naturally start looking for alternatives.

The stock market is one of the few places where ordinary people can still feel that they have a chance to change their lives. All you need is a smartphone, a trading account, and a few taps to enter the game.

For many young people, this feels almost like their last realistic opportunity to close the wealth gap. But, as we've already said, big opportunities often come with big risks. With margin trading, those risks become even larger: gains can be bigger, but losses can come much faster.

The second underlying factor is a famous concept called the 'Korea discount.' Investors use this term to describe the tendency for South Korean stocks to trade at lower valuations than comparable businesses in other developed countries.

Part of the explanation lies in the structure of South Korea's family-controlled conglomerates, known as chaebols. Another part comes from longstanding controversies over the rights of minority shareholders.

What matters here is that many investors believe this discount exists. And if people don't believe the market will adequately reward them for holding stocks over the long term, they become more inclined to chase profits from shorter market swings.

The third factor is short selling. In theory, short selling is a perfectly normal tool in financial markets. It helps identify irrational prices and limits the extent to which stocks become overvalued.

But in the eyes of many South Korean retail investors, short selling is a game played by large institutions, especially foreign funds. Years of scandals involving short-selling practices have only deepened that suspicion.

And when trust in the fairness of the market declines, people become less interested in playing the long game. They want to make money quickly before they become the people someone else profits from.

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, investing has simply become too easy.

Never before in history has betting on the market been so convenient. In the past, if you wanted to trade stocks, you had to call a broker, wait for confirmation, and pay substantial fees.

Now everything is right there on your phone. Buying takes seconds. Selling takes seconds.

On top of that, financial content is everywhere: analysis videos, livestreams, online communities, forums, news reports, recommendations, and predictions. Every day, people are repeatedly told that there is another opportunity in the market.

This stock is about to surge! A new trend is forming! Another rally is coming, everyone!

When all these things come together, the market starts looking more like a social network than a place to invest. Everyone watches price charts, refreshes the news, and follows the crowd's reactions. The more people react, the more people trade.

The entire system has made investing so frictionless that placing a buy order is almost as easy as clicking 'like.' And when making a trade becomes that easy, the number of bad decisions tends to rise as well.

Put those four factors together, and you get a very unusual environment. On one side, there is the pressure to make money fast. On the other, there is the feeling that the rules of the game are not entirely fair. In the middle, there are countless financial tools promising to help people even the odds.

When those conditions exist at the same time, the market inevitably creates products to satisfy that demand.

A striking example is the CFD scandal of 2023. CFD stands for 'contract for difference.' To put it simply, a CFD allows you to speculate on whether an asset's price will go up or down without actually owning the underlying asset.

Like many other speculative products, CFDs often come with substantial leverage. And that can be extremely dangerous.

According to South Korean financial regulators, most of the people participating in CFD trading were actually individual investors. But in trading data, many of their transactions were recorded under the names of institutions or foreign investors.

This distorted the market. Looking at the data, it became difficult to tell who was actually buying, who was selling, and who was ultimately carrying the risk.

When the scandal came to light, regulators responded in the familiar way: they introduced new rules, raised margin requirements, tightened eligibility standards, and stepped up supervision.

But those measures only addressed the symptoms. The underlying desire to speculate remained. Close one door, and people start looking for another.

As CFDs came under tighter restrictions, money flowed into other leveraged products. Some of it moved into overseas markets; some went into financial instruments that were more complicated and harder to supervise.

By the end of 2024, South Korean individual investors had built up substantial holdings of foreign stocks. Among their investments, higher-risk products were becoming increasingly common, including forms of leverage that were already restricted or tightly controlled at home.

In other words, when people couldn't play the game in their own backyard, they went looking for another field.

This is why some experts compare speculation to flowing water. You can block one stream, but the water will find another route. The more firmly you block it, the more it tries to squeeze through smaller gaps. Sometimes it ends up somewhere even harder to control than where it started.

So banning one financial product does not necessarily eliminate the demand for speculation. It may simply push that demand into another form—one that is farther away, more complicated, and less visible.

But everything we've discussed does not mean that South Koreans are inherently greedy or obsessed with getting rich quickly.

When an entire system puts pressure on people every day, choices that look irrational to outsiders can make perfect sense to the people facing those pressures.

Imagine you're a 28-year-old South Korean. Your job isn't stable. Your contract gets renewed one year at a time, and you have no idea what the future holds.

Meanwhile, housing prices are so high that you have no idea when you'll ever be able to afford a home. Every day, you open your phone and see the stock market rising. People are constantly showing off their profits. Stories about ordinary people becoming rich are everywhere.

In those circumstances, staying on the sidelines is not easy.

The scariest thing is not margin trading itself. It's the reasons people turn to margin trading in the first place.

If a generation grows up feeling that working hard may not be enough to live comfortably, that saving for years may still not buy them a decent home, and that following the old rules will only leave them further behind, then the search for riskier options becomes almost inevitable.

It isn't necessarily greed. It's a sense of helplessness, a fear of falling behind, and a belief that if you don't speed up, you'll never catch up.

And here's the irony: in the short term, the market often rewards exactly that way of thinking.

Every time the market falls and then rebounds, leveraged investors feel vindicated. They look at the profits in their accounts and think, 'See? I was right! I'm amazing at investing. Next time, I should go even bigger.'

This is one of the market's most dangerous traps: it makes people confuse luck with skill.

Winning once doesn't make you a stock-market genius. It may simply mean that you happened to be in the right place during favorable market conditions.

One or two successful trades can feel great. But it may only take a few bad ones to wipe out those gains. The debt remains, interest keeps accumulating, and time is rarely on the side of someone using leverage.

At its core, this is still one of the consequences of asset prices rising faster than incomes for a long period.

Debt grows year after year. Asset prices outpace wages. Cheap money sticks around for too long. Capital continuously flows into riskier assets.

Throughout that process, many people convince themselves that they will be the ones who manage to get out before everything goes wrong.

That same belief has appeared in almost every speculative bubble in history. And very often, it is exactly that belief that helps bring everything crashing down.

So when will South Koreans realize what's happening and walk away from this game?

Actually, I think many of them already understand the risks. But when they will step away is another question, and we don't really know the answer yet.

As 2026 unfolds, South Korea's stock market continues to experience intense volatility. On one side, foreign investors keep withdrawing capital. On the other, individual investors continue buying with determination.

People have very different views about where the market is heading. Some believe that stocks are now undervalued and that the future still looks promising. Some are even continuing to borrow money to invest, convinced that the next rebound is just around the corner.

And who knows? Maybe this time they'll be right.

Of course, the optimistic scenario is entirely possible. South Korea is still home to some of the world's leading companies. It has a powerful semiconductor industry, a role in AI, and strong potential drivers of long-term growth.

If conditions develop favorably, the market could absolutely resume its upward climb after this correction.

But opportunity always comes with risk. Leverage remains extremely high. Household debt is still among the highest in the world. Meanwhile, the real economy is recovering more slowly than many had hoped.

When a financial system is loaded with leverage, every shock becomes much more dangerous. It could be a change in global interest rates, a geopolitical crisis, an unexpected development in the semiconductor industry, or simply a sudden shift in market sentiment.

Perhaps the most important question to watch over the coming years is this: can South Korea find a way to help its younger generation build wealth without forcing them to take bigger and bigger gambles?
